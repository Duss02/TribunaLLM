Konstantin Kholodilin of DIW Berlin wrote in 2024 that rent control has existed since World War I and has experienced a renaissance since the 2010s. Designs differ: Berlin's 2020 law froze rents, Oregon's 2019 law caps annual increases at 7 percent plus inflation, and Catalonia's 2020 policy set a reference price for each housing unit.

Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade and Franklin Qian studied a 1994 San Francisco law that extended rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980. In the American Economic Review in 2019 they reported that rent control limited renters' mobility by 20 percent and lowered displacement from San Francisco.

Diamond, McQuade and Qian's 2019 American Economic Review study of San Francisco also found that landlords whose buildings came under rent control reduced rental housing supply by 15 percent, by selling to owner-occupants and redeveloping buildings. The authors concluded that the lost supply likely drove up market rents in the long run.

Diamond, McQuade and Qian compared tenants in San Francisco small multifamily buildings built before 1980, which gained rent control in 1994, with tenants in buildings built from 1980 to 1990, which did not. Their findings come from one city and one law change, so they may not carry over to rent control designed differently or in other markets.

The March 2019 working paper version of Diamond, McQuade and Qian reported that the effects of rent control on staying put were stronger for older households and for households already long resident. With race imputed from names and addresses, it estimated that Black, Hispanic and Asian tenants were 10.7, 10.1 and 6.4 percentage points more likely to remain in San Francisco because of the law, against 2.8 points for white tenants.

David Autor, Christopher Palmer and Parag Pathak studied the 1995 end of rent control in Cambridge, Massachusetts. In the Journal of Political Economy in 2014 they estimated that decontrol added about 1.8 billion dollars to the value of Cambridge's housing stock between 1994 and 2004, nearly a quarter of total residential price appreciation in that period.

Oregon became the first US state with statewide rent control when Governor Kate Brown signed Senate Bill 608 on 28 February 2019, limiting annual increases to 7 percent plus inflation and exempting buildings under 15 years old. California's AB 1482, signed on 8 October 2019 and effective 1 January 2020, caps increases at 5 percent plus inflation or 10 percent, whichever is lower, through 1 January 2030.

In an August 2024 summary of his review of the rent control literature, Konstantin Kholodilin reported that of 65 studies of controlled rents, 56 found that rent control lowered them as intended, 6 found no effect and 3 found a positive effect.

Kholodilin's August 2024 summary also reports that of 16 studies on rental supply, 12 found a negative effect, 3 found none and 1 found a positive effect. Of 17 studies on uncontrolled rents, 14 found that rent control raised them, and of 26 studies on mobility, 25 found that it reduced mobility.

Kholodilin wrote in August 2024 that his review of 206 works on rent control is far from delivering a complete picture of the net effects. He said he cannot guarantee the sample is complete, that older, unpublished and non-English studies may be underrepresented, and his counts tally studies by the direction of their findings.

In a 7 February 2012 survey of the IGM Forum panel of economists, panelists were asked whether local rent-increase limits such as those in New York and San Francisco had improved the amount and quality of broadly affordable rental housing over three decades. Only one panelist agreed.

Economist Richard Arnott argued in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 1995 that the theoretical case against the "soft" rent control systems of North America is weak, and that the empirical case against them is weak too. He urged economists to evaluate current systems case by case rather than oppose them across the board.

A study by DIW Berlin researchers including Konstantin Kholodilin and Sofie Waltl, published on 24 February 2021, found that advertised rents for regulated Berlin apartments were about 11 percent lower than for unregulated ones right after the rent cap took effect on 23 February 2020. The cap covered an estimated 95 percent of the rental market.

DIW Berlin's February 2021 study of Berlin's rent cap also found that the supply of advertised rental apartments fell by more than half, while rents in nearby Potsdam rose by 12 percent. A 2024 Management Science paper by Anja Hahn and coauthors reported that a share of the lost listings appeared to be permanently lost to the rental sector.

The DIW Berlin analysis of Berlin's rent cap examined rental advertisements only through 21 March 2020, about one month after the cap took effect, to avoid distortions from the coronavirus pandemic. Germany's Federal Constitutional Court declared the Berlin law void in a decision dated 25 March 2021 and published on 15 April 2021, ruling that Berlin lacked legislative competence.

Joan Monras and Jose Montalvo studied a rent cap that Catalonia introduced in September 2020 in some municipalities. In a September 2023 working paper for the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco they found that rental prices dropped approximately 5 percent in treated municipalities relative to untreated ones.

The Monras and Montalvo working paper on Catalonia's rent cap also found that the overall supply of rental units in the market declined around 10 percent. Low-price units saw price increases above those in untreated municipalities, while high-price units saw price declines.

Kenneth Ahern and Marco Giacoletti found that St. Paul's 2021 rent control law, which capped annual increases at 3 percent, reduced property values by 6 to 7 percent, an aggregate loss of 1.6 billion dollars. In their 2022 NBER working paper, the tenants who gained most had higher incomes and were more likely to be white, while the owners who lost most had lower incomes and were more likely to be minorities.

Ahern and Giacoletti measured St. Paul's price effect using home sales that closed from November 2021 through January 2022, compared with sales in adjacent cities. St. Paul's council amended the ordinance in September 2022, and the city now exempts newly constructed rental properties, so the policy they studied differs from the one now in force.

About one million New York City households live in rent-controlled or rent-stabilized apartments, according to the city's Department of Housing Preservation and Development. New York State's Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act, signed on 14 June 2019, made the state's rent regulation laws permanent and ended high-rent vacancy deregulation, under which more than 300,000 units had been deregulated since 1994.

New York City's 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, presented to the City Council in March 2024, found a citywide net rental vacancy rate of 1.41 percent. The rate was below 1 percent for units renting for less than 1,650 dollars, and the median asking rent was 3,000 dollars against a median renter income of 70,000 dollars.

On 25 June 2026 New York City's Rent Guidelines Board voted 7 to 1 to set 0 percent increases on both one-year and two-year leases for roughly 1 million rent-regulated apartments. James Whelan of the Real Estate Board of New York said the vote would worsen the housing crisis, while Mayor Zohran Mamdani called it a historic victory for tenants.
