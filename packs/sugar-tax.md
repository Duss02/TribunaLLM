The World Health Organization's 2015 guideline on sugars recommended that adults and children reduce their daily intake of free sugars to less than 10 percent of total energy intake, and conditionally suggested a further reduction to below 5 percent, or roughly 25 grams per day. WHO noted that a single can of sugar-sweetened soda contains up to 40 grams of free sugars.

The United Kingdom's Soft Drinks Industry Levy was announced in the March 2016 Budget and came into law in April 2018. It charges producers 18 pence per litre on drinks with 5 grams to under 8 grams of sugar per 100 millilitres and 24 pence per litre at 8 grams or more, with no levy below 5 grams.

Berkeley, California was the first US jurisdiction to tax sugar-sweetened beverages, at 1 cent per ounce. Philadelphia became the second US city to do so in January 2017, at 1.5 cents per ounce, and Seattle began a tax of 1.75 cents per ounce on 1 January 2018.

Mexico introduced an excise tax of 1 peso per litre on sugar-sweetened beverages on 1 January 2014, roughly a 10 percent price increase. A 2016 BMJ study of 6,253 households found purchases of taxed beverages were 6 percent lower in 2014 than expected without the tax, and 12 percent lower by December 2014. Reductions were larger in low-income households, at 9 percent over 2014 and 17 percent by December 2014.

A report published on 1 December 2022 by the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities, which took over the sugar reduction programme from Public Health England, covered drinks subject to the UK levy. In retailer and manufacturer-branded products, the sales-weighted average sugar content fell from 3.8 grams per 100 millilitres in 2015 to 2.1 grams in 2020, a decrease of 46.0 percent, while total sugar sold fell 34.3 percent and volume sold rose 21.3 percent.

A January 2023 PLOS Medicine study by Nina Rogers and colleagues used National Child Measurement Programme data on over 1 million English schoolchildren. It associated the UK levy with a 1.6 percentage point absolute reduction in obesity prevalence among Year 6 girls aged 10 to 11, and a 2.4 percentage point reduction in the most deprived quintile.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in JAMA Network Open by Tatiana Andreyeva and colleagues found 86 eligible articles, with 62 studies in the meta-analysis. It estimated that taxes were passed through to prices at a rate of 82 percent and that sales of taxed sugar-sweetened beverages fell by a mean of 15 percent, and its narrative synthesis found no negative changes in employment.

A 2019 systematic review in Obesity Reviews by Teng and colleagues pooled 17 evaluations of real-world sugar-sweetened beverage taxes across 6 jurisdictions. It estimated that a tax equivalent to a 10 percent price increase was associated with an average 10.0 percent decline in purchases and dietary intake of taxed beverages.

A 2019 JAMA study by Christina Roberto and colleagues compared Philadelphia with Baltimore after Philadelphia began taxing sweetened beverages at 1.5 cents per ounce in January 2017. Volume sales of taxed beverages in Philadelphia chain retailers fell by 51.0 percent in 2017, from 2.475 billion ounces to 1.214 billion ounces.

A 2024 JAMA Network Open study by Jennifer Jones-Smith and colleagues followed 6,313 children using health records. After Seattle's 1.75 cents per ounce tax took effect on 1 January 2018, Seattle children had a 0.90 percentage point larger decrease in body mass index, measured as a percentage of the 95th percentile, than children in nearby comparison areas.

A 2016 systematic review in Public Health Nutrition by Kathryn Backholer and colleagues included eleven articles from high-income countries. Of seven studies reporting weight outcomes after sugar-sweetened beverage price increases, all reported either similar weight reductions across socioeconomic groups or greater reductions for lower socioeconomic groups.

On 2 July 2025 the World Health Organization launched its 3 by 35 initiative, calling for real prices of tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks to rise by at least 50 percent by 2035 through health taxes. WHO projected that a one-time 50 percent price increase could prevent 50 million premature deaths over 50 years.

In the same 2019 JAMA study of Philadelphia's tax, sales in Pennsylvania zip codes bordering Philadelphia rose by 308.2 million ounces, from 713.1 million to 1.021 billion. This offset 24.4 percent of the decrease in Philadelphia, and the data covered chain retailers and about 25 percent of the ounces of taxed beverages sold in Philadelphia.

A 2025 JAMA Pediatrics study by Emily Gregory and colleagues analysed health records of 136,078 youth aged 2 to 18 in Philadelphia and surrounding counties. Two years after the city's beverage tax began, it found no association with changes in standardized body mass index or obesity prevalence.

John Cawley and colleagues surveyed Philadelphia and comparison-area residents before the tax and nearly one year after, in a 2019 Journal of Health Economics study. The tax cut adults' frequency of soda consumption by 31 percent but had no detectable impact on children's consumption of soda or all taxed beverages, and Philadelphia residents increased purchases of taxed beverages outside the city.

The 2023 PLOS Medicine study of the UK levy found no change in obesity prevalence among Year 6 boys, apart from a 1.6 percentage point increase in the least deprived quintile, and no overall change among reception children aged 4 to 5. The authors noted that interrupted time series analyses are vulnerable to unidentified cointerventions and time-varying confounding, and said strategies beyond beverage taxation will be needed.

The 2022 Andreyeva review found that changes in sugar-sweetened beverage consumption were not significant, even though sales of taxed beverages fell. It reported a heterogeneity statistic of 100 percent for the sales estimate, found cross-border shopping in most studies of local-level taxes, and noted that data on outcomes across subpopulations were limited.

The 2016 Backholer review found that all five studies examining the average household amount paid in sugar-sweetened beverage tax reported that it would be regressive. Low-income households were estimated to pay 0.10 to 1.0 percent of annual income in the tax, against 0.03 to 0.60 percent for high-income households, a difference the authors described as small.

In a 2016 American Journal of Public Health study of Berkeley's 1 cent per ounce tax, Jennifer Falbe and colleagues interviewed 990 people before the tax and 1,689 after, in low-income neighborhoods of Berkeley, Oakland and San Francisco. Reported consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages fell 21 percent in Berkeley and rose 4 percent in comparison cities, but follow-up came about 4 months after implementation and relied on a beverage frequency questionnaire.

The 2016 Mexico study measured only beverages bought from stores, from January 2012 to December 2014, and its authors called for monitoring of longer-term purchases, substitution and health implications. It found that purchases of untaxed beverages were 4 percent higher than expected, mainly because of more bottled plain water.

A 2024 Lancet Regional Health - Americas study by Petimar and colleagues examined adults aged 18 to 65 in Philadelphia and comparison areas using health records. In a panel of 175,675 adults the estimated change in body mass index three years after the tax was -0.32 kg/m2, with a confidence interval of -0.85 to 0.20, and the authors described the evidence of a decrease as limited and called for replication.
