The first reactor to feed electricity to a grid was the Soviet AM-1 at Obninsk, which began generating in June 1954 with a design output of 5 megawatts electric. The World Nuclear Association records that Calder Hall in Britain began operating in 1956 and Shippingport in Pennsylvania in 1957.

The International Energy Agency reported in January 2025 that nuclear power produces just under 10 percent of global generation and is the second-largest source of low-emissions electricity after hydropower. It also counted 63 reactors with more than 70 gigawatts of capacity under construction, one of the highest levels since 1990.

The International Energy Agency's Global Energy Review 2025 reported that global electricity generation grew by over 1,200 terawatt-hours in 2024, with solar photovoltaics adding about 480 terawatt-hours, the most of any source, and wind about 180. Nuclear output increased by nearly 4 percent, and nuclear covered 9 percent of global generation.

The IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (Working Group III, Annex III, 2014) gives median life-cycle emissions of 12 grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour for nuclear power. The same table gives 820 for coal, 490 for gas combined cycle, 48 for utility-scale solar photovoltaics and 11 for onshore wind.

The IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (2014) also reports wide ranges around its median life-cycle emissions: 3.7 to 110 grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour for nuclear power, 740 to 910 for coal and 18 to 180 for utility-scale solar photovoltaics. A median therefore summarizes a spread of published estimates that depend on each study's assumptions.

A 2021 life-cycle assessment published by the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe found nuclear power at 5.1 to 6.4 grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour, the lowest of the technologies it assessed. The same report gave 8 to 83 for solar photovoltaics and 403 to 513 for natural gas.

The World Nuclear Association reported that reactors worldwide generated 2,667 terawatt-hours in 2024, passing the previous record of 2,660 terawatt-hours set in 2006, at an average capacity factor of 83 percent. It estimated that this output avoided 2.1 billion tonnes of CO2 compared with equivalent coal generation.

Kharecha and Hansen (Environmental Science and Technology, 2013) calculated that global nuclear power prevented an average of 1.84 million air pollution-related deaths and 64 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions between 1971 and 2009. Their method assumed that all nuclear output would otherwise have been supplied by coal and natural gas.

Sovacool and colleagues (Nature Energy, October 2020) analyzed data on 123 countries over 25 years. They found that larger national nuclear programs did not tend to be associated with significantly lower carbon emissions, while larger renewable programs did, and that nuclear and renewable programs showed a negative association with each other.

Fell, Gilbert, Jenkins and Mildenberger (Nature Energy, January 2022) reanalyzed national data and reported that nuclear power and renewable energy are both associated with lower per capita CO2 emissions, with effects of similar magnitude and statistical significance. Their paper responded to the 2020 study by Sovacool and colleagues.

Germany shut more than half of its nuclear production capacity after the 2011 Fukushima accident and closed its last three reactors on 15 April 2023. Jarvis, Deschenes and Jha (Journal of the European Economic Association, 2022) found that coal and net imports mainly replaced the lost output, at a social cost of 3 to 8 billion euros per year, mostly from air-pollution mortality risk. An earlier working paper put the cost near 12 billion dollars per year.

France launched its Messmer plan in 1974 after the oil crisis. The World Nuclear Association reports that nuclear supplied 67 percent of French generation in 2024 (380 of 570 terawatt-hours) and that over 80 percent of French electricity comes from nuclear or hydro.

At the Barakah plant in the United Arab Emirates, construction of Unit 1 began in July 2012 and Unit 4 entered commercial operation on 5 September 2024. The four units total 5,600 megawatts and supply up to 25 percent of the country's electricity.

Our World in Data, in an article last updated in July 2022, gave death rates per terawatt-hour of about 24.6 for coal, 2.8 for gas and 0.07 for nuclear, drawing on Markandya and Wilkinson (2007) and Sovacool et al. (2016). Its nuclear figure assumes 433 deaths from Chernobyl and 2,314 from Fukushima.

The Chernobyl Forum reported in 2005 that about 50 emergency workers died of radiation sickness and its consequences, and expected around 4,000 premature deaths among the 600,000 people most heavily exposed. A 2006 study by the International Agency for Research on Cancer projected about 16,000 cancer deaths in Europe by 2065, with a 95 percent uncertainty interval of 6,700 to 38,000.

Plant Vogtle units 3 and 4 in Georgia were originally expected to cost 14 billion dollars and begin commercial operation in 2016 and 2017, according to the US Energy Information Administration. Unit 4 entered commercial operation on 29 April 2024. The Associated Press calculated the owners' projected cost, including financing, at 31 billion dollars, or nearly 35 billion dollars counting 3.7 billion dollars paid by Westinghouse.

France's Flamanville 3 reactor was connected to the grid on 21 December 2024, about 17 years after construction began in 2007 under a planned 54-month schedule. In January 2025 the Cour des comptes put its total cost to completion, including financing, at 23.7 billion euros in 2023 euros, against an initial estimate of 3.3 billion euros.

EDF said on 20 February 2026 that Hinkley Point C unit 1 would start in 2030, 12 months later than its 2029 target, citing electromechanical work, and put the project cost estimate at 35 billion pounds in 2015 prices. The estimate at approval was 18 billion pounds in 2015 prices.

Finland's Olkiluoto 3 reactor began construction on 12 August 2005 under a 3.2 billion euro turnkey contract signed in December 2003. Regular electricity production began on 16 April 2023, against a start originally expected in mid-2009.

Lazard's Levelized Cost of Energy+ report (version 19.0, July 2026) put unsubsidized US nuclear at 175 to 255 dollars per megawatt-hour. It gave 40 to 98 for utility-scale solar, 37 to 99 for onshore wind and 51 to 129 for gas combined cycle. Lazard based its nuclear range on public cost estimates for Vogtle units 3 and 4.

Lazard's 2026 Levelized Cost of Energy+ report states that its analysis assumes 60 percent debt at an 8 percent interest rate and 40 percent equity at 12 percent, and that its nuclear range reflects Vogtle unit 3 at the high end and unit 4 at the low end. It says it does not address externalities such as greenhouse gases and, except where illustrated, does not consider the intermittent nature of some renewables or the grid impacts of adding them.

The US Government Accountability Office reported in September 2021 that about 86,000 metric tons of commercial spent nuclear fuel were stored at 75 sites in 33 states, growing by roughly 2,000 metric tons a year. The Department of Energy discontinued its effort to license the Yucca Mountain repository in 2010.
