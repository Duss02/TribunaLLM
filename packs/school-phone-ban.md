UNESCO's Global Education Monitoring Report team reported in March 2026 that 114 education systems, representing 58 percent of countries, had a national ban on mobile phones in schools. The same monitoring found that 24 percent of countries had such bans in June 2023 and 40 percent in early 2025.

The Department for Education in England first published mobile phone guidance for schools on 19 February 2024. The guidance became statutory on 29 June 2026, and schools are expected to follow it from 1 September 2026.

In a study published in Labour Economics in 2016, economists Louis-Philippe Beland and Richard Murphy surveyed schools in four English cities about their phone policies and linked the answers to exam records. They found that high-stakes exam results rose by about 0.07 standard deviations after schools banned phones, with the gains driven by the lowest-achieving students.

A paper dated 13 May 2026 by Guilherme Lichand, Luca Moreno-Louzada, Thiago da Costa and Matthew Gentzkow studied Rio de Janeiro, where phones were banned in classrooms in August 2023 and school-wide in February 2024. Middle schools that lacked strict phone rules before the ban improved a combined math and Portuguese test index by 0.06 standard deviations relative to similar schools that already had strict rules.

Economists David Figlio and Umut Ozek studied a large urban school district in Florida that required phones to be silenced and kept out of sight all day from the 2023-24 school year. In their October 2025 National Bureau of Economic Research paper, spring test scores in the second year of the ban were 1.1 percentiles higher than the spring test just before the ban, and unexcused absences fell.

Economist Sara Abrahamsson studied Norwegian middle schools that banned smartphones, in a paper published online in the Journal of Human Resources in March 2026. She found no average effect on education or mental health, but for girls the bans reduced use of psychological healthcare and raised grades, and bullying fell for both boys and girls.

A 2025 monitoring report by the Kohnstamm Institute and Oberon for the Dutch education ministry covered the national agreement that took effect in secondary schools on 1 January 2024. Among 271 secondary schools that had a phone policy, 74.9 percent reported a positive or very positive effect on pupils' concentration, and none reported a negative effect.

In a Pew Research Center survey of U.S. public school teachers fielded in fall 2023, 72 percent of high school teachers said student distraction by cellphones was a major problem in their classroom. The share was 33 percent among middle school teachers and 6 percent among elementary school teachers.

The OECD's PISA 2022 results, published in 2023, found that on average across OECD countries 25 percent of students said they were distracted by other students using digital devices in most or every mathematics lesson. Students who reported this in at least some lessons scored 15 points lower in mathematics than those who said it never or almost never happened, after accounting for socioeconomic profile.

A study of U.S. schools adopting Yondr lockable phone pouches by Hunt Allcott, Matthew Gentzkow and colleagues, circulated as NBER Working Paper 35132 in 2026, used a teacher survey. The share of students reported to use phones in class for personal reasons fell from 61 percent to 13 percent after adoption, and high school math scores rose by about 0.024 student-level standard deviations.

A Pew Research Center survey of 5,110 U.S. adults, conducted from 30 September to 6 October 2024, found that 68 percent support banning cellphones for middle and high school students during class. Among supporters, 91 percent called fewer distractions in class a major reason.

The SMART Schools study, led by Victoria Goodyear and published in The Lancet Regional Health - Europe in February 2025, compared 1,227 pupils aged 12 to 15 in 30 English secondary schools, 20 with restrictive phone policies and 10 with permissive ones. It found no evidence of a difference in mental wellbeing between the groups, although pupils in restrictive schools reported 0.67 hours less phone use during the school day. The study was cross-sectional, so it could not establish causation.

Economists Dany Kessel, Hulda Lif Hardardottir and Bjorn Tyrefors partly replicated the 2016 England study with Swedish data, reaching a school survey response rate of about 75 percent, and published it in Economics of Education Review in 2020. They found no impact of mobile phone bans on student performance and could reject even small gains.

The 2026 study by Allcott and colleagues of lockable phone pouches in U.S. schools found average effects on test scores that were consistently close to zero over the first three years after adoption. High schools saw modest positive effects, middle schools saw small negative effects, and the study found no clear evidence of improvements in self-reported classroom attention or perceived online bullying.

In the same pouch study, disciplinary incidents rose in the year of adoption by about 0.03 student-level standard deviations, roughly a 15 percent increase in suspension rates, before fading. Student-reported well-being fell by roughly 0.2 standard deviations in the first year and then rose by 0.16 standard deviations, a pattern the authors interpreted cautiously because the panel was short.

In the Florida district studied by Figlio and Ozek, the suspension rate in the month after disciplinary enforcement began was 25 percent higher than in the same month a year earlier. Their estimates showed in-school suspensions about 30 percent higher among Black students in the first year, with the effects mostly dissipating overall in the second year.

A 2024 scoping review by Marilyn Campbell and colleagues in the Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools included 22 studies on phone bans. The authors noted an absence of randomized controlled trials and said the evidence rested on a small number of studies with different designs, samples, definitions of bans and outcome measures.

In the same Pew Research Center survey of U.S. public school teachers from fall 2023, 82 percent said their school or district had a cellphone policy. Among teachers with a policy, 30 percent overall and 60 percent of high school teachers said it was very or somewhat difficult to enforce.

In the 2025 Kohnstamm Institute and Oberon monitoring of the Dutch national phone agreement, 27.7 percent of the 271 secondary schools with a policy reported a positive or very positive effect on pupils' learning results, and the rest were neutral or could not judge. The questionnaire was usually completed by directors, department heads or team leaders, so the findings are self-reported judgments rather than test data.

The same Pew Research Center survey of 5,110 U.S. adults, conducted from 30 September to 6 October 2024, found that 36 percent support banning phones during the entire school day, while 53 percent oppose an all-day ban. Support for an all-day ban was also 36 percent among K-12 parents.

In the Rio de Janeiro study, principals reported that most or all students were on phones during class at 19 percent of treatment schools and 15 percent of control schools after the ban, so restrictions were imperfectly implemented. The authors noted that their phone-use measures came from principals' retrospective reports, which may be affected by social-desirability bias.
