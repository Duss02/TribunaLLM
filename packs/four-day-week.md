In 1926 Ford Motor Company announced a five-day, 40-hour week for its US plants, according to a 2024 Library of Congress account. The US Fair Labor Standards Act, signed in June 1938, required overtime pay beyond 44 hours a week in its first year, beyond 42 in its second year and beyond 40 afterward.

In February 2022 Belgium passed a labor reform that lets full-time employees ask to compress their hours into four days with no change in total weekly hours or pay, according to the Society for Human Resource Management. A 38-hour week would become four days of 9.5 hours, so this differs from trials that cut hours while keeping pay.

The UK four-day week pilot ran from June to December 2022 with 61 companies and about 2,900 employees, who kept 100 percent of pay in exchange for a meaningful cut in work time. In a February 2023 report by the research group Autonomy with academics at Boston College and the University of Cambridge, 56 of the 61 companies (92 percent) were continuing the four-day week and 18 had made it permanent.

In the same Autonomy report on the 2022 UK pilot, employee surveys before and after the trial showed that 39 percent of employees were less stressed and 71 percent had lower burnout at the end. Average weekly hours fell from 38 to 34, and 15 percent of employees said no amount of money would make them accept a five-day schedule again.

Autonomy's 2023 report on the UK pilot found that resignations at participating companies fell from 2.0 to 0.8 per 100 employees, a 57 percent drop, compared with the same months a year earlier. Sick and personal days per employee per month fell from 2.0 to 0.7, a 65 percent drop.

On revenue, Autonomy's UK pilot report found a weighted average rise of 1.4 percent from the start to the end of the 2022 trial among 23 companies. Among 24 other companies compared with a prior-year period, revenue was on average 34.5 percent higher, and employee numbers across 34 firms dipped 1.3 percent.

Autonomy's 2023 UK pilot report said 70 companies signed up, 9 did not begin the trial, and 66 percent of the 61 participants had 25 or fewer employees. The authors said they could not say the trends in resignations, hiring and absence were statistically significant, citing small numbers, and that participants were recruited by campaign groups and asked for a small donation toward costs.

The same Autonomy report found that new hires at UK pilot companies fell from 3.4 to 2.4 per 100 employees, a 37 percent drop. Average hours fell by 4 hours rather than to 32, and while 71 percent of surveyed employees reported fewer hours, 15 percent reported working more and 13 percent saw no change.

A study by Wen Fan, Juliet Schor, Orla Kelly and Guolin Gu, published in Nature Human Behaviour in July 2025, followed 2,896 employees at 141 organizations in six countries through a six-month four-day week with no pay cut. Burnout, job satisfaction, mental health and physical health improved, a pattern not seen in 12 control companies.

The 2025 Nature Human Behaviour four-day week study by Fan, Schor, Kelly and Gu lists several limits. Companies volunteered rather than being randomly assigned, health and well-being were self-reported by employees, and the sample leaned toward professional workplaces in English-speaking countries.

Trials run by Reykjavik City Council and the Icelandic government between 2015 and 2019 cut hours for more than 2,500 workers, about 1 percent of Iceland's working population, from 40 to 35 or 36 hours a week at the same pay. A 2021 report by Autonomy and the Association for Sustainable Democracy said productivity and service provision stayed the same or improved at most trial workplaces, and that about 86 percent of Iceland's workforce has since gained shorter hours or the right to request them.

In August 2019 Microsoft Japan gave staff special paid leave on all five Fridays under its Work-Life Choice Challenge 2019 Summer. The company reported that sales per employee rose 39.9 percent compared with August 2018, electricity use fell 23.1 percent and 92.1 percent of staff rated the four-day week positively.

Microsoft Japan's 39.9 percent figure for August 2019 was sales divided by number of employees for a single month, compared with August 2018. Working days that month were 25.4 percent lower than a year earlier, and the measure is revenue per head rather than output per hour.

In March and April 2018 New Zealand trust company Perpetual Guardian ran an eight-week four-day week for all 240 staff at full pay. Professor Jarrod Haar of Auckland University of Technology found job performance was maintained and work-life balance improved, and the company's leadership team reported broadly no change in company outputs.

The Bennett Institute for Public Policy at the University of Cambridge analyzed South Cambridgeshire District Council's four-day week trial for about 450 office-based staff, which ran from January to March 2023. Of 16 services' performance indicators, 9 improved significantly against the same period in 2022 and 7 stayed similar or slipped slightly, and none fell to a level of concern.

On June 30, 2023 UK local government minister Lee Rowley wrote to South Cambridgeshire District Council asking it to end its four-day week trial immediately. He said councils must show value for money and that paying employees for an extra day of work that is not carried out was unlikely to demonstrate it.

Utah moved state employees to a compressed schedule of four 10-hour days in 2008 under its Working 4 Utah initiative. A July 2010 legislative audit found that expected utility savings of $3 million came to just over $500,000, and the state ended the schedule in 2011 after reports of smaller savings than hoped and resident complaints about Friday access.

At the Svartedalens care home in Gothenburg, Sweden, nurses moved to six-hour days at full pay in a trial that began in February 2015. Euronews reported in January 2017 that it needed 17 additional staff at a cost of 1.26 million euros and that the city council, with a new majority, did not extend it, though the home reported less sick leave.

A 2008 study in the journal Economic Policy by Marcello Estevao and Filipa Sa examined France's cut in the legal workweek from 39 to 35 hours, phased in from 2000. It found that aggregate employment was not significantly affected, employment among directly affected workers declined, and some people took second jobs or moved to smaller firms where hours were not constrained.
