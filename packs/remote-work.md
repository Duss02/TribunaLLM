The US Census Bureau's American Community Survey estimated that 5.7 percent of US workers usually worked from home in 2019, 17.9 percent in 2021, 15.2 percent in 2022 and 13.8 percent in 2023. That is about 9 million people in 2019 and more than 22 million in 2023.

The Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes, an online survey by Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Shelby Buckman and Steven Davis, estimated that about 27 percent of paid full days in the US in December 2025 were worked from home. Across 2025 it found that among full-time employees aged 20 to 64, 12 percent were fully remote, 61 percent worked fully on site and 27 percent were hybrid.

On September 16, 2024 Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told corporate employees to work in the office five days a week from January 2, 2025, after a three-day requirement that began on May 1, 2023. He said the three-day policy had strengthened the company's conviction about the benefits of being together, including collaboration, learning and culture.

In January 2025 JPMorgan Chase told employees on hybrid schedules to return to the office five days a week from March 2025, according to an internal memo reported by Reuters. The bank has about 310,000 employees, more than half of whom already came in five days a week, and the memo called the policy the best way to run the company.

On January 20, 2025 President Donald Trump signed a Memorandum on Return to In-Person Work. It directed heads of federal executive departments and agencies, as soon as practicable, to end remote work arrangements and require employees to work in person at their duty stations full time, with exemptions at agency heads' discretion.

A study by Michael Gibbs, Friederike Mengel and Christoph Siemroth, published in the Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics in 2023, used data on over 10,000 professionals at HCL Technologies, an Indian IT services firm, after its March 2020 switch to working from home. Hours worked rose, including an 18 percent rise in work after normal business hours, and productivity measured as output per hour fell 8 to 19 percent.

Natalia Emanuel and Emma Harrington studied call centers at a US Fortune 500 firm with 344 remote and 1,592 on-site workers in a Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report in May 2023. Before COVID-19, remote workers answered 12 percent fewer calls per hour, and when offices closed the productivity of formerly on-site workers fell 4 percent relative to already-remote workers. Remote work was also linked to lower call quality and lower promotion rates.

Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington and Amanda Pallais studied software engineers at a Fortune 500 online retailer from 2019 to 2024 in NBER Working Paper 31880, revised in June 2026. Sitting near teammates raised coding feedback by 18.3 percent, with gains concentrated among younger and less-tenured engineers, but experienced engineers wrote less code when sitting near teammates.

A Nature Human Behaviour study led by Longqi Yang of Microsoft, published online in September 2021, analyzed more than 61,000 US Microsoft employees before and after the company's March 2020 move to firm-wide remote work. The share of collaboration time spent with connections outside an employee's own group fell by about 25 percent, and employees added fewer new collaborators.

Melanie Brucks and Jonathan Levav reported in Nature in 2022 on a laboratory study and a field experiment with employees of a company with offices in five countries. Pairs working face to face generated more creative ideas than pairs on video calls, while there was no evidence that video pairs were worse at selecting which idea to pursue.

Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han and James Liang published a randomized trial in Nature in 2024 of 1,612 university-educated employees at Trip.com in Shanghai. For six months some worked five days in the office and others three days in the office and two at home, assigned by birthday; quit rates fell 33 percent in the hybrid group, with no difference in performance reviews or promotions over the next two years.

The Trip.com experiment covered one company in Shanghai and compared five office days with a hybrid week of three office days, not with fully remote work. The fall in quit rates was significant for non-managers, women and employees with long commutes, but managers' quit rates did not change significantly.

In a 2015 Quarterly Journal of Economics study, Nicholas Bloom, James Liang, John Roberts and Zhichun Jenny Ying randomly assigned volunteer call-center employees at the Chinese travel agency CTrip to work from home or the office for 9 months. Home working raised performance by 13 percent, but promotion rates conditional on performance fell, and when employees were later allowed to choose, gains rose to 22 percent.

Fortune reported in January 2024 on a working paper by Yuye Ding and Mark Ma covering 137 S&P 500 firms that announced return-to-office mandates, using Glassdoor employee reviews and financial data. It found significant declines in employee job satisfaction after the mandates but no significant change in financial performance or firm value.

A November 2024 working paper by Yuye Ding, Zhao Jin, Mark Ma, Betty Xing and Yucheng Yang used LinkedIn-based employment histories of over 3 million employees at 54 S&P 500 technology and finance firms. Turnover rose 14 percent after return-to-office mandates, more for women, senior and highly skilled employees, and the firms took longer to fill vacancies.

The authors of the November 2024 return-to-office brain drain paper noted that the vast majority of the 54 firms did not have full five-day mandates, with 10 having moved to five days by November 2024. They also noted that Microsoft management said its internal data did not support an earlier finding that senior Microsoft employees left after its mandate.

Alexandre Mas and Amanda Pallais reported in the 2017 American Economic Review, using a discrete choice experiment in the hiring process of a national call center, that the average worker was willing to give up 8 percent of wages for the option to work from home.

The Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes reported in January 2026 that employers' plans for work from home averaged near 1.4 days per week and had been similar to actual work from home since mid-2022. It also reported that the gap between the days employees want at home and the days employers plan fluctuates near half a day.

The call-center study by Emanuel and Harrington came from a single US Fortune 500 firm, and it found that an 8 percent productivity gap persisted after formerly on-site workers went remote, which the authors attributed mostly to less productive workers choosing remote jobs. The Gibbs, Mengel and Siemroth study covered one Indian IT firm that moved abruptly to working from home in March 2020 because of the pandemic.

The Microsoft collaboration study by Yang and colleagues covered only US employees at one large technology company during the abrupt March 2020 shift to remote work. Its authors used employees who were already remote before the pandemic as a comparison group to separate the effect of remote work from the effects of the pandemic.
