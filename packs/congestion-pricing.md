London's Congestion Charge began on 17 February 2003 at 5 pounds per day for driving in a central zone. Transport for London now lists a daily charge of 18 pounds, in effect since 2 January 2026, payable for driving in the zone from 7:00 to 18:00 Monday to Friday and from 12:00 to 18:00 on weekends and bank holidays.

Transport for London reported in April 2004 that traffic entering the charging zone during charging hours was 18 percent lower than before charging in 2002. It also reported that congestion within the zone had fallen by 30 percent since charging began on 17 February 2003.

Transport for London reported in August 2008 that cars entering the original zone were 21 percent lower than before charging, yet congestion had returned to pre-charging levels. It attributed this to water and gas main replacement works and traffic management measures that reduced road capacity, and said congestion would be worse without the charge.

Transport for London's April 2004 annual report on congestion charging estimated that the charge was directly responsible for reductions of approximately 12 percent in emissions of nitrogen oxides and fine particles (PM10) from road traffic within the zone. It also estimated a 19 percent reduction in traffic-related carbon dioxide emissions.

Stockholm ran a congestion charging trial from 3 January to 31 July 2006, held a referendum in September 2006, and reintroduced the charges permanently in August 2007. Borjesson and colleagues reported in 2012 that crossing the cordon cost 2 euros at peak hours, with a maximum of 6 euros per day, converting at 10 Swedish kronor per euro.

A 2012 paper by Maria Borjesson and colleagues in Transport Policy found that traffic across the Stockholm cordon was 20 to 22 percent below the 2005 level in March to June 2006. Traffic was 19 percent below 2005 in 2010 and 20 percent below in 2011, and during the trial 24 percent of car commuting trips across the cordon disappeared, nearly all switching to public transport.

Jonas Eliasson of KTH Royal Institute of Technology reported in 2014 that Stockholm's charging system cost about 1.9 billion Swedish kronor in total, including first-year operations. He wrote that yearly revenues were about 14 percent below the forecast made before the start, mainly because more vehicles were exempt than expected.

Eliasson's 2014 overview reproduces a Stockholm cost-benefit analysis showing an annual net social benefit of about 654 million kronor after 220 million kronor of operating costs, with start-up costs recouped in about 4 years. The same table puts travelers' consumer surplus at minus 279 million kronor per year, because the 804 million kronor of charges paid exceeds the value of their time savings.

Borjesson and colleagues reported in 2012 that, since 2007, tax deductibility has cut the effective charge by 60 percent for about 8 percent of car trips across the Stockholm cordon. Company cars, 23 percent of vehicles crossing, receive at least a 60 percent reduction because drivers can pay from gross salary, and no such discounts applied during the 2006 trial.

Emilia Simeonova, Janet Currie, Peter Nilsson and Reed Walker compared central Stockholm with other Swedish city centres and found that the congestion tax reduced ambient air pollution by 5 to 15 percent. Their NBER working paper, revised in September 2019, estimated that acute asthma visits among children aged 0 to 5 fell by 16 percent during the 2006 trial.

Simeonova, Currie, Nilsson and Walker reported that they saw no corresponding changes in accidents or in hospitalizations for nonrespiratory conditions among young children in Stockholm. In an additional analysis of children younger than six months old, their asthma estimates lose conventional statistical significance once all fixed effects are included.

Borjesson and colleagues reported that support for permanent charges in Stockholm fell to 36 percent just before the 2006 trial and rose to 52 percent once it began. In the September 2006 referendum 53 percent of city voters, excluding blank votes, backed keeping the charges, though a tally of all votes across the county, where only some municipalities held referendums, showed a majority against.

An equity analysis of Stockholm by Franklin and coauthors, summarized in Eliasson's 2014 overview, estimated an average welfare change of minus 78 kronor per person per year if charge revenue is not returned. The estimate was plus 180 kronor with a lump-sum refund and plus 173 kronor with an income tax cut, and the analysis reported little significant difference between income categories.

Edinburgh residents rejected a plan to charge 2 pounds to cross a cordon in a postal referendum held from 7 to 21 February 2005, with 133,678 votes against and 45,965 in favor, or more than 74 percent against. The council had proposed spending the revenue on public transport.

Milan's Area C charges 5 euros for most vehicles entering an 8.2 square kilometer central zone on weekdays from 7:30 to 19:30. Matthew Gibson and Maria Carnovale used an eight-week court suspension in 2012 as a natural experiment and found in the Journal of Urban Economics in 2015 that the charge reduced vehicle entries by 14.5 percent and air pollution by 6 to 17 percent.

Gibson and Carnovale also found that Milan drivers shifted trips to unpriced times, just before 7:30 a.m. or after 7:30 p.m., and drove around the boundary of the priced zone. They reported that the effect on traffic varied with public transit availability, and that entries by charged vehicles fell 0.3 percent for each 1 percent increase in the price.

New York's Central Business District Tolling Program began at midnight on 5 January 2025. According to the MTA, passenger vehicles with E-ZPass pay 9 dollars at peak times, 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. on weekdays, to enter Manhattan at and below 60th Street, with overnight rates 75 percent lower, and the peak toll is scheduled to rise to 12 dollars in 2028 and 15 dollars in 2031.

MTA Bridges and Tunnels reported in January 2026 that in the first year of New York's congestion pricing, 27 million fewer vehicles entered the Central Business District, an 11.7 percent reduction. The agency said the program generated more than $550 million in net revenue to advance $15 billion in transit improvements.

A Cornell University study published on 8 December 2025 in npj Clean Air analyzed daily data from 42 air quality monitors over 518 days. It found that fine particulate pollution in Manhattan's Congestion Relief Zone fell 22 percent during the first six months of 2025, compared with a projected baseline.

The Cornell study's 22 percent figure compares measured concentrations with a projected baseline of 13.8 micrograms per cubic meter, so it depends on a modeled counterfactual, and it covers six months in one city. Senior author Oliver Gao compared it with 5 to 15 percent for Stockholm between 2006 and 2010 and 7 percent for London between 2019 and 2022.

The office of New York Governor Kathy Hochul reported on 5 January 2026 that morning rush speeds at major crossings into the zone were up an average of 23 percent, and 51 percent at the Holland Tunnel. Analyst Charles Komanoff wrote in Vital City on 2 January 2026 that taxi and Uber data suggested speeds within the zone were barely above a year earlier, though speeds had been falling about 5 percent a year before the toll.
