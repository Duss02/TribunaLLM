Directive 2000/84/EC sets the current EU rules: clocks in all member states move forward one hour on the last Sunday of March and back one hour on the last Sunday of October, according to a 2023 European Parliament Think Tank briefing.

In the United States the Uniform Time Act of 1966 set the framework for daylight saving time and let states exempt themselves; Hawaii did so in 1967 and Arizona in 1968. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 moved the period to run from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November, the Congressional Research Service reported in 2019.

A European Commission online consultation in 2018 received about 4.6 million replies, of which 84 percent favored ending the twice-yearly clock change and 16 percent wanted to keep it. On 12 September 2018 the Commission proposed a directive to end seasonal time changes.

On 26 March 2019 the European Parliament voted 410 to 192, with 51 abstentions, to end seasonal clock changes, and it moved the target date to 2021. EU governments meeting in the Council have not agreed a common position, so the clock changes continue.

On 20 October 2025 Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez announced that Spain would ask the EU to end clock changes, saying they barely save energy and harm health. In March 2026 Commission spokeswoman Anna-Kaisa Itkonen said a coordinated solution was still possible and that a study to support the decision should conclude by the end of 2026.

On 14 July 2026 the US House of Representatives passed H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act, by 308 votes to 117 to make daylight saving time permanent. CBS News reported that President Trump said he would sign it and that the Senate, which passed a similar bill by unanimous consent on 15 March 2022, had yet to act.

The United States tried year-round daylight saving time once. A law enacted on 15 December 1973 during the oil crisis started it on 6 January 1974, but Congress ended the trial early and clocks returned to standard time on 27 October 1974 after concern about darkness on winter mornings.

Russia moved to permanent summer time in 2011. A return to permanent standard time took effect on 26 October 2014 after approval by the Federation Council, and Global News reported that a survey found fewer than a third of Russians approved of the 2011 change.

Mexico's Senate voted 59 to 25, with 12 abstentions, on 26 October 2022 to end daylight saving time, with an exception for towns along the US border. Health Secretary Jorge Alcocer backed a return to standard time, while critics cited less evening daylight for exercise and possible disruption to financial markets, Fox News reported.

Swedish researchers Imre Janszky and Rickard Ljung reported in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2008, using Swedish data from 1987 onward, that heart attack incidence rose by about 5 percent in the first week after the spring clock change. They also found a lower risk on the Monday after the autumn change, though the weekly reduction was smaller than the spring increase.

Fritz and colleagues reported in Current Biology in 2020, using 732,835 fatal US road crashes from 1996 to 2017, that the spring change to daylight saving time raised fatal crash risk by 6 percent, with no effect from the autumn change. Economist Austin Smith estimated in the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics in 2016 that the spring transition caused over 30 US road deaths a year across 2002 to 2011, at a social cost of $275 million annually.

Barnes and Wagner reported in the Journal of Applied Psychology in 2009 that on the Monday after the spring change US workers slept on average 40 minutes less than on other days. Using 1983 to 2006 mining data, they found more frequent and more severe injuries on those Mondays.

In 2024 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine a position statement that the United States should eliminate seasonal time changes in favor of permanent standard time, which it said aligns best with human circadian biology. It first published such a position in 2020.

Matthew Kotchen and Laura Grant used more than 7 million household billing observations from Indiana and reported in the Review of Economics and Statistics in 2011 that daylight saving time increased residential electricity demand by about 1 percent, because savings on lighting were outweighed by extra heating and cooling.

A US Department of Energy report to Congress in October 2008 estimated that the four extra weeks of daylight saving time in 2007 saved about 1.3 terawatt-hours of electricity, or 0.03 percent of 2007 US consumption of 3,900 terawatt-hours. It found changes in gasoline use and traffic volume were not statistically significant.

Energy findings differ by place and method. The Department of Energy's statistical estimate of 2007 savings carried a margin of plus or minus 40 percent at 95 percent confidence, and its utilities were not randomly selected. Ryan Kellogg and Hendrik Wolff found in 2008 that Australia's extension of daylight saving time for the 2000 Sydney Olympics did not conserve electricity.

Jennifer Doleac and Nicholas Sanders reported in the Review of Economics and Statistics in 2015 that robberies fell by 7 percent after the shift to daylight saving time, with the largest effects in the hours affected by the extra evening light. They estimated that the 2007 extension saved $59 million a year in avoided robbery costs.

Calum Cunningham and colleagues reported in Current Biology in 2022, using 1,012,465 US deer-vehicle collisions, that the autumn change to standard time was followed by a 16 percent spike in such collisions. Their model predicted year-round daylight saving time would prevent 33 human deaths and $1.19 billion in collision costs annually, while permanent standard time would add $2.39 billion.

In a 2016 working paper, Weily Toro, Robson Tigre and Breno Sampaio used a difference-in-differences design on Brazilian data and estimated that firearm homicides fell by roughly 9.83 percent during daylight saving months, concentrated in the early evening hours. They estimated that the clock shift saved about 5,035 potential victims from 2006 to 2015.

Christian Bunnings and Valentin Schiele estimated in the Review of Economics and Statistics in 2021 that darkness causes road accident costs of more than 500 million pounds a year in Great Britain. They estimated that setting daylight saving time year-round could save 8 percent of those costs.

A Gallup poll of 1,001 US adults on 21 to 27 January 2025 found 54 percent wanted to do away with daylight saving time and 40 percent favored it. Asked which arrangement they preferred, 48 percent chose standard time all year, 24 percent daylight saving time all year and 19 percent the current switching; each question went to about 500 respondents, with a margin of error of 6 percentage points.

Health evidence varies in size and strength. A Finnish study by Sipila and colleagues covering 2004 to 2013 found ischemic stroke admissions rose 8 percent in the first two days after clock changes, but the whole-week rise of 3 percent was not statistically significant. A Michigan study by Jiddou and colleagues of 935 heart attack patients found a ratio of 1.17 for the week after the spring change, with a confidence interval of 1.00 to 1.36.
